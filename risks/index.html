What a month can cost

The invoice arrives even when the barangay does not pay.

A full site can absorb a bad week. A new site cannot absorb the same week, because the satellite bill is already larger than a small book. This page prices the months the guide’s ₱37,600 profit does not describe, then projects the cash a rollout actually needs.

Standing rates follow the model: ₱500 a user, Local Priority 1 TB at ₱8,400, host ₱2,000, upkeep ₱2,000, capital ₱150,000.

Risks that show up in a month

01

The data plan runs out

Most busy months

Local Priority 500 GB is a start tier. A barangay that watches video can finish it before the cycle ends. Upgrades apply next billing cycle, not the same afternoon.

Response: Read usage every week. Move to 1 TB before you sell the 80th voucher. Do not promise unlimited video on 500 GB.

Cost signal: ₱3,750 more to move from ₱4,650 to the ₱8,400 1 TB plan — or a wave of non-renewals if speeds collapse.

02

Collections come in thin

Any month, worse at the start

The guide counts 100 paying users. A new site more often has 25, then 40, then 60. Prepaid vouchers also leak: shared logins, unsold cards, a seller who settles late.

Response: Sell through the host and one sari-sari. Don’t hire for 100 users until the vouchers are actually moving.

Cost signal: Each missing user is ₱400–₱600. An 8% leak on a full ₱50,000 book is ₱4,000. A 40-user book is a different business.

03

Power is the real backbone

Brownout season, and random Tuesdays

The dish, the PoE, and the radio die together when the barangay supply dies. A generator drinks fuel. A solar kit that was sized for a sunny week goes flat in a string of rain days.

Response: Prefer a site with power you would trust with a freezer, plus a UPS. If you would not, put solar and battery in the ₱15,000–₱40,000 power budget before launch, not after the third outage.

Cost signal: Fuel and a revisit often add ₱1,500–₱6,000. A dark day on a full site gives up roughly ₱1,670 of revenue. The satellite invoice does not shrink.

04

A truck roll

A few times a year, clustered in bad weather

Someone has to climb the roof: a shifted dish, a flooded enclosure, a radio that rebooted into silence, a CPE at the far house. Remote barangays turn a one-hour fix into a day.

Response: Train one or two people in the area. Leave a spare PoE and a spare cable on site. Prepare the mount and the power before the crew arrives so the first visit is the only install visit.

Cost signal: Fuel, food, a local tech, and lost hours: about ₱1,500–₱8,000 a visit. The guide’s ₱2,000 upkeep line does not cover a hard month of these.

05

A storm month

Typhoon season — one bad week, sometimes two

Rain fade on 5 GHz, a dish nudged off the sky, an antenna that needs re-aiming, and customers who skip a renewal because the week was unusable.

Response: Guy the pole. Use a proper mount, not a nailed board. Shut down cleanly if a signal is posted. Keep the arrestor and the enclosure closed.

Cost signal: Repairs of ₱3,000–₱25,000 plus a thinner book. On the projection, month 8 drops a full site to 40 paying users and an ₱8,000 repair.

06

Lightning and surge

A handful of months, uneven by province

A nearby strike kills the PoE, the router, or the outdoor radio. The dish is less often the victim than the cheap injector under it.

Response: Arrestor, a real ground, and drip loops. A spare injector is cheaper than a second trip.

Cost signal: ₱1,500 for an injector up to ₱20,000 if the outdoor radio dies. The projection puts a ₱6,000 hit in month 11.

07

Theft and tampering

Occasional, then contagious if one site is easy

Batteries, radios, and client radios walk. A host who is casual about the gate makes it worse.

Response: You own the gear, and the contract says so. Lock the enclosure. The host’s fee is partly for eyes on the roof.

Cost signal: ₱6,000–₱25,000 to replace a battery or a radio, plus the days the sector is dark.

08

The host changes their mind

Usually after the first calm months

They want a higher fee, a relative hired, or the dish off the roof because of a family dispute. Without a paper, you are negotiating from their ladder.

Response: Written terms: free internet, a fixed fee, access, security, and termination. You take the equipment with you.

Cost signal: ₱500–₱2,000 more a month, or a relocation: another truck roll plus a new mount. Worst case, a dark site while you move.

09

Someone else shows up

A promo month, or a new tower

A mobile promo or a later fiber build gives people a reason to stop renewing. The honest defense is that you picked a barangay the incumbents still skip.

Response: Survey demand before you mount. Keep the price inside ₱400–₱600. Don’t build the second site in a barangay that already has a stable signal.

Cost signal: A drop from ₱500 to the ₱400 floor is ₱10,000 less revenue at 100 users. Losing 30 users is ₱15,000.

Three kinds of month

A dark day on a full book gives up about ₱1,667 of revenue. Ten dark days is about ₱16,667. The bill for Starlink and the host does not move. Break-even at today’s standing costs is 25 paying users.

Not monthly, but they can empty a month

Billing before the paper exists

NTC VAS / DTIP registration is the license to charge people. Core fees are about ₱6,000–₱10,000, plus possible minimum capitalization. A stop-order costs more than the fee. Mayor’s permit, BIR, and barangay clearance are the local layer.

Residential Starlink cannot be resold

The guide is explicit: use Local Priority / Business. A residential plan that “works fine in the pilot” is the thing that unwinds the pilot.

The four-month payback assumes a full site

Capital comes back in about four calm months only if 100 people are already paying. A site that fills over seven months pays back later.

One thousand barangays is an execution plan

Eighteen to twenty-four months to 1,000 sites is a crew, a permit rhythm, and a capital account. It is not a booked pipeline. Pilot 10–30 sites first.
